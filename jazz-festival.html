The Lowcountry Jazz Festival · Since 2009

Culture and care go together.

How a family physician and his daughter turned a Labor Day weekend of world-class jazz into a scholarship engine for future healthcare workers—and the main source of funding for Closing the Gap in Healthcare.

01 · The why

Not enough doctors who looked like the patients.

Dr. Thaddeus Bell was one of the first African American graduates of the Medical University of South Carolina. Decades into his career as a family physician, he kept seeing the same problem: there weren’t enough Black physicians serving rural and underserved communities—and for many talented students, the barrier was money.

He had already spent years teaching people how to understand their own health. Now he wanted to help the next generation of caregivers get through school. The question was how to raise the money, year after year, in a way the whole community would show up for.

The answer was music.

[Photo: Dr. Bell and Tonisha — family-approved]

02 · A father and daughter

Built by two Bells.

Dr. Bell founded the Lowcountry Jazz Festival in 2009 with his daughter, Tonisha Bell-Alston. Tonisha was also instrumental in building Closing the Gap in Healthcare—it was her idea to take her father’s health tips online through the Health-E-Newsletter.

Tonisha passed away from cancer in 2015. The festival carries her name in its story and her purpose in every scholarship it funds.

03 · How it grew

From a fundraiser to a Charleston tradition.

  1. 2009
    The first festival
    Dr. Bell and Tonisha launch a Labor Day weekend jazz fundraiser for scholarships for African American students at MUSC.
  2. 2015
    Losing Tonisha
    Tonisha passes away from cancer. The festival continues in her honor.
  3. 2016
    A producer joins
    Tony Clarke, a veteran artist manager and concert presenter, becomes producer and artistic director.
  4. 2018
    10th festival, new home
    The festival moves from the North Charleston Performing Arts Center to the Charleston Gaillard Center, and adds a community health summit with Black Health Matters.
  5. 2021
    Back on stage
    The 12th festival returns for Labor Day weekend.
  6. Today
    A Charleston tradition
    Scholarships now reach students in any of MUSC’s six colleges.

04 · Where the money goes

Every ticket does three jobs.

01

Scholarships for future caregivers

The festival began by funding scholarships for African American students at MUSC. Today, scholarships support students in any of MUSC’s six colleges—the next generation of doctors, nurses, and health professionals.

02

Health education that makes sense

The festival is the primary source of funding for Closing the Gap in Healthcare—paying for Dr. Bell Says videos, barbershop health talks, radio tips, and community events across South Carolina.

03

Health on the festival stage

The festival weekend has also brought health to the crowd, including a community health summit with national experts hosted with Black Health Matters.

“Culture and care go together. Both require community.”

— Dr. Thaddeus J. Bell, Founder, Lowcountry Jazz Festival

Both great music and great medicine require the same thing: showing up for your community with excellence, year after year.
